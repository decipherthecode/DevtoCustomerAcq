## Section 1: Document Basics

### What is this document?
The Business_Model.md is your product's "constitution." It answers the most important questions about your business in one place:
- **What** problem you're solving
- **Who** you're solving it for
- **How** your product solves it (especially with AI)
- **How** you make money
- **How** you find customers
- **Whether** it's working (metrics)

### Why write it down?
Most founders keep this in their heads — and then wonder why their team is confused, their investors aren't interested, or they're building the wrong features. Writing it down forces clarity.

### How often should I update it?
- **Sections 1–4** (product, problem, solution, customer): Update when your strategy changes significantly (every 2–3 months early-stage)
- **Sections 7–9** (revenue, GTM, competition): Update when you change pricing, launch a new channel, or a new competitor appears
- **Sections 11–12** (milestones, KPIs): Update every 2 weeks during your sprint review
- **Section 13**: Update monthly

### Who should fill this in?
Ideally the founder — with input from anyone on the team who has a relevant view. Don't outsource this to a consultant or AI without reviewing every word yourself. You need to believe everything in this document.

## Section 2: Product Identity

### 2.1 Product Name
- Pick a name that is easy to spell, say, and remember.
- Avoid made-up words that mean nothing (e.g., "Xyloflo"). Prefer names that hint at what the product does.
- Check: Is the `.com` domain available? Is the Twitter/X handle available?

### 2.2 Tagline
- A tagline is NOT a description — it's a hook. It should create curiosity or make a promise.
- **Test:** Read your tagline to someone who doesn't know your product. Do they want to know more?
- Bad tagline: *"A project management tool for founders"* (boring, descriptive)
- Good tagline: *"Your AI copilot for getting work done — automatically."* (creates curiosity)

### 2.3 One-Line Description
- Use the formula exactly: `[Product] helps [WHO] to [DO WHAT] so that [RESULT].`
- The WHO must be specific (not "everyone" or "businesses")
- The RESULT must be meaningful — what changes for the customer?

### 2.4 Elevator Pitch
- Imagine you're in an elevator with your dream customer. You have 30 seconds before they get off.
- Cover: (a) the problem, (b) your solution, (c) why it's better, (d) who it's for, (e) the scale of the opportunity
- Write it conversationally — not like a press release

### 2.5 Product Stage
- Be honest about where you are. If you say you're "launched" when you're not, you'll misalign expectations.

## Section 3: Problem Statement

### Why this section matters
This is the most important section of the entire document. If you get the problem wrong, everything else falls apart — your solution, your marketing, your pricing, everything. Spend more time here than anywhere else.

### 3.1 The Core Problem
**The #1 mistake founders make:** Describing their *solution* when asked to describe the *problem.*
- Wrong: *"There are no good AI project management tools."* (This describes a lack of solution, not a real problem)
- Right: *"Solo founders lose 15 hours a week on admin tasks that don't generate revenue."* (This describes a real, felt pain)

**How to find the real problem:**
1. Interview at least 10 people in your target customer group
2. Ask: "Tell me about a time you struggled with [area]." Don't ask "Would you use a product that..."
3. Listen for emotions: frustration, embarrassment, loss, wasted time, wasted money

### 3.2 Who Feels This Problem?
- Be specific. "Everyone" is not an answer.
- The more specific you are, the better your marketing will be.
- A common beginner mistake: being too broad because you're afraid of limiting yourself.

### 3.3 Current Workarounds
**Why this matters:** If there are no workarounds at all, either the problem isn't real or people don't care enough to fix it. If there are workarounds, they are your real competition — not just the direct competitors you think of.

**How to find workarounds:** Ask your customer interview subjects, *"What do you do today when you hit this problem?"*

### 3.4 Cost of the Problem
Investors and customers are more persuaded by numbers than by stories. If you can say *"this problem costs founders $78,000 a year,"* that's far more compelling than *"it's really frustrating."*

Sources for data:
- Industry reports (Statista, McKinsey, Forrester)
- Surveys of your own customers or community
- Public forum threads (Reddit, Indie Hackers, Hacker News)
- Your own experience (if you are your own target customer)

### 3.5 Jobs-To-Be-Done Statement
JTBD is a way of thinking invented by Clayton Christensen. The core idea: people don't buy products — they "hire" them to make progress in their life.

The statement format is:
> *"When I [SITUATION], I want to [MOTIVATION], so I can [EXPECTED OUTCOME]."*

**SITUATION** = the trigger moment (when does the problem happen?)
**MOTIVATION** = what the person wants to do/feel/avoid
**EXPECTED OUTCOME** = what success looks like

## Section 4: Solution & AI Value Proposition

### 4.1 The Solution
- Describe your solution in plain language. Avoid technical jargon.
- **Test:** Can a 10-year-old understand what your product does from this description?

### 4.2 The AI Advantage
This is critical for AI products. Many tools claim to "use AI" but don't explain *why* AI makes it meaningfully better. You need to be clear about:

1. **What specific AI capability** you're using (LLM, computer vision, agents, etc.)
2. **What it enables** that was previously impossible or impractical
3. **Why humans or traditional software can't do this as well**

**Good AI advantage statement:** *"AI agents can read and understand the context of 50 open GitHub issues in seconds — a task that would take a human 2 hours every day."*

**Weak AI advantage statement:** *"We use AI to make the product smarter."*

### 4.3 The Magic Moment
Every successful product has a moment when a new user thinks, *"OK, this is actually amazing."* This moment should happen **within the first session** — ideally within 10 minutes of signing up.

**How to find yours:** Ask beta users, *"When did you first feel like this product was going to work for you?"*

**Why it matters for your business model:** The faster you get users to the magic moment, the better your activation rate will be — and activation is the single biggest predictor of retention.

### 4.4 Value Proposition Canvas
This is a tool from Strategyzer (Alex Osterwalder). It forces you to map your solution directly to your customer's pains and gains.

Key insight: **Pain relievers ≠ features.** A pain reliever is the *outcome* a feature creates. For example:
- Feature: "AI auto-syncs your GitHub"
- Pain Reliever: "Never have to manually update your task board again"

## Section 5: Target Customer

### 5.1 Ideal Customer Profile (ICP)
The ICP is a description of the perfect type of customer for your product. It is NOT a description of a single person (that's a Persona) — it's a description of a *type* of company or person.

**How to define your ICP:**
1. Look at your best current customers (most engaged, happiest, paying the most)
2. What do they have in common?
3. Those common attributes = your ICP

**Why this matters for your business model:** Your ICP drives everything else — your pricing (what they can afford), your GTM (where to find them), your features (what they need most).

### 5.2 Customer Personas
A persona is a fictional character who represents your ICP. Think of it as putting a face and a story on your ICP.

**The purpose of a persona:** When making decisions (what to build, how to price, what to write in your marketing), you ask yourself: *"Would Alex find this valuable? Would Alex understand this messaging?"*

**Common persona mistakes:**
- Making them too generic ("28-year-old professional who likes technology")
- Not including their fears (fear is a huge motivator for purchasing decisions)
- Not knowing where they hang out online (this determines your GTM channels)

### 5.3 Market Size (TAM/SAM/SOM)
**Why investors care about this:** They want to know if your market is big enough to build a significant business.

**How to calculate TAM:** Start with a known industry report. E.g., "The global project management software market is $15B."

**How to calculate SAM:** Narrow it to the segment you can actually reach. E.g., "We target English-speaking solo founders using AI tools — roughly $800M of that market."

**How to calculate SOM:** What can you realistically capture in 2 years? Multiply your target customer count by your price. E.g., "10,000 customers × $33/month × 12 months = ~$4M ARR."

**Honesty note:** Investors know these estimates are rough. What they're looking for is logical thinking, not perfect precision.

## Section 6: AI Features & Agent Workflows

### 6.1 Core Feature List
**The "launch priority" column is critical.** Most early-stage products fail because they try to build too much. The Launch Priority column forces you to decide:
- 🔴 MVP: Must exist before you launch. Not having this = no product.
- 🟡 Beta: Important but can launch without it. Build it during beta.
- 🟢 Post-Launch: Nice to have. Build it after you're making money.

**Common mistake:** Marking too many features as 🔴 MVP. Be ruthless. A working MVP should have 2–3 core features, not 10.

### 6.2 AI Agent Workflow Map
An AI agent workflow is different from a regular feature. It involves:
1. A **trigger** (what starts the agent)
2. **Data collection** (what the agent reads or gathers)
3. **AI processing** (the LLM or model that makes a decision)
4. **An action or output** (what the agent produces or does in the world)
5. **Human review** (optional — should the human approve actions?)
6. **A feedback loop** (does the agent learn from outcomes?)

**Why the feedback loop matters:** This is what makes AI products better over time. Without it, your AI doesn't improve — it's just a static tool.

**Safety note:** For any AI agent that takes actions in the world (sending messages, updating files, making purchases), always include a human review step in your MVP. Trust is built slowly; don't give AI agents too much autonomy before you've earned user trust.

### 6.3 AI Stack
**Model-agnostic design tip:** Try to design your product so you can swap one AI provider for another (e.g., switch from Claude to GPT-4) without rebuilding the entire product. This protects you from pricing changes or outages from a single vendor.

**Cost warning:** AI API costs can grow unexpectedly as you scale. Make sure your pricing accounts for the AI cost per user.

## Section 7: Revenue Model & Pricing

### Choosing your pricing model
For SaaS AI products, **subscription** is almost always the right starting point. It creates predictable revenue, aligns incentives with customer success, and is easy for customers to understand.

**Usage-based pricing** can be very compelling for AI products (customers pay per AI action), but it makes revenue less predictable. Consider usage-based as an *add-on* to a base subscription, not as the sole model.

### 7.2 Pricing Tiers — How Many?
**Recommendation for early-stage:** Start with **2 tiers** (Free/Starter + one paid tier). Add a third tier once you understand which features enterprise or power users value most.

**The "sweet spot" for SaaS pricing:**
- $9–19/month: Very low barrier; low revenue per customer; you'll need lots of customers
- $29–49/month: The most common solo-founder SaaS tier; good balance
- $79–149/month: Positioning as a professional tool; fewer customers needed
- $200+/month: Enterprise territory; usually requires a sales process

**Annual pricing:** Offer a 15–20% discount for annual payment upfront. This improves cash flow and reduces churn.

### 7.3 Unit Economics — A Beginner's Guide

**CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost):**
- Total money spent on sales & marketing in a month ÷ number of new customers gained = CAC
- Example: $600 spent on ads + $100 on content = $700. Gained 10 customers. CAC = $70.

**LTV (Lifetime Value):**
- ARPU ÷ Monthly Churn Rate = LTV
- Example: $33 ARPU ÷ 3% churn = $1,100 LTV

**LTV:CAC Ratio:**
- The golden benchmark is **3:1 or higher**
- If LTV:CAC is below 1:1, you're losing money on every customer — this is existential

**Churn Rate:**
- If you're losing more than 5% of customers every month, fix this before spending money on acquisition
- Reducing churn from 8% to 3% is worth far more than doubling your acquisition rate

## Section 8: Go-To-Market Strategy

### Why GTM strategy is not "post and pray"
Many founders build a product, post it on Product Hunt, and then wonder why no one signed up. GTM is a deliberate, repeatable system for finding, attracting, and converting customers.

**The key principle:** Don't try to be everywhere. Pick 2–3 channels and go deep.

### Content / SEO — The Long Game
- SEO takes 6–12 months to show results. Start it on Day 1.
- Your best SEO content answers questions your ideal customers are already asking.
- How to find these questions: Google "best [problem] tools", look at "People Also Ask," check Reddit/Quora, use Ahrefs or Google Search Console.
- **Don't write generic content.** Write opinionated, specific content that only you (as someone living this problem) can write.

### Product-Led Growth — Let the Product Sell Itself
PLG works best when:
- Your product creates immediate visible value (magic moment happens fast)
- There is a natural reason to invite others (collaboration features, shareable output)
- The free tier is genuinely useful — not crippled

**The "made by" viral loop:** Adding a small "Made with [Your Product]" tag to any output your product creates (reports, documents, summaries) is the simplest PLG loop. A potential customer sees the output → they click the tag → they land on your site.

### Outbound / Cold Outreach — The Short Game
Cold outreach gets bad results when it's generic. It works when it's hyper-personalised and reaches people who actually have the problem.

**The formula for good cold outreach:**
1. Observe something specific about the person (a tweet, a product they launched, a post they wrote)
2. Connect it to their likely pain
3. Make a low-friction ask (not "buy my product" — ask "would this be useful to you?")

**Volume vs. quality:** 10 deeply personalised messages will outperform 100 generic ones every time.

## Section 9: Competitive Landscape

### Why honest competitor analysis matters
Founders often make two mistakes:
1. **"We have no competitors"** — This is almost never true. If there are truly no competitors, there is probably no market.
2. **"We're better at everything"** — This destroys trust with sophisticated readers. Every competitor is better at something.

### How to rate competitors honestly
Use the 1–5 scale:
- **5** = Best in class; customers choose this competitor specifically for this feature
- **4** = Very good; clear advantage
- **3** = Adequate; gets the job done
- **2** = Weak; customers complain about this area
- **1** = Missing or broken

### The Positioning Statement
The positioning statement is not your tagline — it's a precise, strategic statement of where you sit in the market relative to competitors. It's primarily for internal clarity, not external marketing.

**The goal:** Be the best option for a specific type of customer in a specific situation. Don't try to be the best for everyone.

## Section 10: SWOT Analysis

### How to do a SWOT that's actually useful
Most SWOT analyses are vague and useless (e.g., "Strength: We have a great team"). A useful SWOT is:
- **Specific:** Not "strong product" — "Proprietary training data from 50 B2B customers that no competitor has"
- **Honest:** List real weaknesses, not trivial ones like "we're sometimes too passionate"
- **Actionable:** Each quadrant should suggest a strategic response

### Turning SWOT into Strategy
Once you have your four quadrants, ask:
- **S + O:** How can I use my strengths to seize opportunities? (Offensive plays)
- **S + T:** How can my strengths help me defend against threats? (Defensive plays)
- **W + O:** How can I fix weaknesses to capture opportunities? (Improvement priorities)
- **W + T:** How can I minimise weaknesses to avoid threats? (Damage control)

## Section 11: Risk Register

### Why documenting risks makes you stronger, not weaker
Investors and experienced founders are not reassured by a founder who says "everything is going great." They're reassured by a founder who has clearly thought through the risks and has a mitigation plan for each one.

### AI-Specific Risks — A Deeper Dive

**Hallucinations:**
AI language models sometimes generate confident-sounding but wrong answers. For AI-powered products, this can destroy user trust instantly.
- **Mitigation:** Human review steps, confidence thresholds, factual verification (RAG/retrieval), clear disclaimers in the UI

**Data Privacy:**
If your product handles user data and sends it to an LLM API, you need to ensure:
- You have permission to process that data (in your Terms of Service + Privacy Policy)
- You're compliant with GDPR/CCPA requirements
- The AI provider's data handling is compatible with your obligations

**Prompt Injection:**
Malicious users can sometimes craft inputs that make your AI system behave in unintended ways.
- **Mitigation:** Input sanitisation, system prompt hardening, output filtering, monitoring

### Regulatory Compliance — Minimum Viable Compliance

**GDPR (if you have any EU users):**
- Add a cookie consent banner
- Write a Privacy Policy that describes what data you collect and how
- Provide a way for users to request their data be deleted
- Don't send EU user data to countries without adequate protection without safeguards

**EU AI Act (if you sell AI products in the EU):**
- Most consumer AI SaaS falls into the "Limited Risk" category
- You need to: be transparent that the user is interacting with AI; mark AI-generated content as AI-generated; not use banned practices (subliminal manipulation, social scoring, etc.)

## Section 12: Development Lifecycle Milestones

### Why "Done When" definitions matter
A milestone without a clear definition of "done" will never be completed — because everyone has a different idea of what done looks like.

**Bad:** "MVP built"
**Good:** "Core features coded and working locally — new user can sign up, connect GitHub, and receive their first AI briefing in < 10 minutes"

### The 500-Customer Journey — What to Expect

**0–10 customers:** Every customer is manual. You onboard them personally, answer support personally, and beg them to give you feedback. This is normal and necessary.

**10–50 customers:** You start to see patterns. Some features are used constantly; others never. Your biggest job is figuring out why people churn.

**50–100 customers:** You have enough data to make decisions. Your onboarding should now be self-serve. Referrals start happening. Content starts generating organic traffic.

**100–500 customers:** This is where systems matter. Your GTM is becoming repeatable. You're hiring or considering hiring. Analytics are driving decisions. Unit economics are becoming clear.

## Section 13: Success Metrics & KPIs

### The North Star Metric — Choosing Wisely
Your North Star Metric (NSM) should be:
- **Directly linked to customer value** (not a vanity metric like page views or signups)
- **A leading indicator** of revenue (it predicts future revenue before it shows up in MRR)
- **Understandable** to your whole team

**Good NSMs for AI SaaS products:**
- "Weekly active users who use the AI feature at least 3 times"
- "Number of AI-generated reports shared externally"
- "Users who complete the automation workflow at least once per week"

**Bad NSMs:**
- Total registered users (vanity — includes inactive users)
- Page views (too far from value)
- Total revenue (a lagging indicator — tells you what happened, not what will happen)

### The AARRR Framework (Pirate Metrics)
The KPI sections in the template follow the AARRR framework:
- **A**cquisition: Getting people to your site/product
- **A**ctivation: Getting them to experience value for the first time
- **R**etention: Keeping them coming back
- **R**evenue: Converting them to paying customers
- **R**eferral: Getting them to tell others

Most founders over-invest in Acquisition and under-invest in Retention. **Fix retention before you scale acquisition.**

### Product-Market Fit Signal
The Sean Ellis test: Ask users, "How would you feel if you could no longer use [Product]?"
- Very Disappointed
- Somewhat Disappointed
- Not Disappointed

If **40% or more** choose "Very Disappointed," you have product-market fit. Below 40% = keep iterating.

## Section 14: Team & Roles

### Solo Founder — How to Think About This Section
If you're a solo founder, this section documents what *you* are responsible for — and helps you plan what to outsource or hire for first.

**Prioritise your first hire based on your weakest area:**
- If you're a strong engineer but weak at marketing → hire/contract marketing first
- If you're a strong marketer but weak at engineering → hire/contract engineering first
- Don't hire a "generalist" early — hire someone who fills a specific gap

### Contractors vs. Full-Time Employees
For the first 6 months, prefer contractors over full-time employees:
- Lower commitment; easier to end if it's not working
- No benefits, equity, or employment law complexity
- Good for testing roles before committing

## Section 15: Cost & Resource Overview

### AI API Cost Warning
AI API costs (Claude, GPT-4, etc.) scale with usage. This means as you get more customers, your costs grow. Make sure your unit economics account for this.

**Rule of thumb:** AI API costs should be **less than 20%** of your ARPU. If ARPU is $33, AI API costs per customer per month should be less than ~$6.50.

**How to calculate AI cost per customer:**
1. Estimate how many AI requests/tokens one customer uses per month
2. Multiply by the API cost per token/request
3. This = your AI COGS per customer

### Break-Even — The Most Important Number
Break-even tells you: *"How many customers do I need to cover my costs?"*

If your break-even is 14 customers and you're at 50, you're profitable at the operating level — congratulations. If you're at 5 customers and break-even is 50, you have a real problem.

**Runway** is how long you can operate before you run out of money. Always know your runway. If it drops below 6 months, treat it as a crisis and act immediately.

## Section 16: Product Roadmap & Sprint Planning

### Quarterly Themes — Why They Help
A quarterly theme (one word like "Foundation," "Activation," "Growth") gives the entire quarter a narrative. It helps you decide what fits and what doesn't. If a task doesn't serve the quarter's theme, deprioritise it.

### Epic vs. Story vs. Task — The Hierarchy

```
Epic (Large — takes weeks/months)
  └── Story (Medium — takes days)
        └── Task (Small — takes hours)
```

**Example:**
```
Epic: "Morning Briefing Agent"
  └── Story: "GitHub Integration"
        └── Task: "Set up GitHub OAuth"
        └── Task: "Write API query to fetch open issues"
        └── Task: "Test with 3 real repos"
  └── Story: "LLM Ranking Engine"
        └── Task: "Write task-scoring prompt"
        └── Task: "Integrate Anthropic Claude API"
        └── Task: "Test with mock data"
```

### Sprint Velocity — Knowing What You Can Realistically Do
Velocity = how many hours/tasks you can realistically complete in a 2-week sprint.

**First sprint:** Estimate conservatively. You'll learn your real velocity after 2–3 sprints.

**Golden rule:** **Never** put more than 80% of your available hours in a sprint. Life happens. Buffer is not waste — it's planning.

### Sprint Retrospective — Don't Skip This
The retro is not about blame. It's about learning. Even 15 minutes of honest reflection after each sprint will compound into significantly better execution over time.

## Section 17: Investor One-Pager

### When to Share This
- Applying to accelerators (Y Combinator, Techstars, etc.)
- Cold emailing angel investors
- Reaching out to advisors
- Networking events (have it ready to send as a follow-up)

### The 10-Slide Pitch Story
Every great pitch follows a story arc:
1. **Conflict** (Problem): Something is broken in the world
2. **Hero** (Your Customer): Someone suffering from that problem
3. **Magic Weapon** (Your Solution): How AI makes this solvable now
4. **The Map** (Market): How big the opportunity is
5. **Treasure** (Revenue Model): How you capture value
6. **Evidence** (Traction): Proof it's working
7. **Battle Plan** (GTM): How you'll scale
8. **Enemies** (Competition): Who else is fighting for this market
9. **Fellowship** (Team): Why you and your team will win
10. **Quest** (The Ask): What you need to succeed

### What Investors Actually Care About
At pre-seed / seed stage (which is where the 0–500 customer journey sits):
1. **Team** — Are these the right people to solve this problem?
2. **Problem** — Is this a real, significant problem?
3. **Traction** — Is there any early evidence this is working?
4. **Market** — Is it big enough to build a venture-scale business?

Notice that a perfect product or perfect technology is NOT on this list. Early-stage investors bet on people and problems, not perfect products.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. ❌ **Being too vague** — "We serve businesses" is not a customer definition. Be specific.
2. ❌ **Confusing solution and problem** — Describe what hurts, not what you built.
3. ❌ **No "Done When" on milestones** — Milestones without completion criteria never get completed.
4. ❌ **Too many MVP features** — Your MVP should do one thing incredibly well, not ten things adequately.
5. ❌ **Ignoring churn** — Every customer you keep is one you don't have to re-acquire.
6. ❌ **Not knowing your unit economics** — If you don't know CAC and LTV, you don't know if you have a business.
7. ❌ **Skipping the competitor analysis** — "We have no competitors" is a red flag to investors and a blind spot for you.
8. ❌ **Treating the roadmap as a promise** — A roadmap is a current best guess. It will change. That's OK.
9. ❌ **Giving AI agents too much autonomy** — Start with human review on all AI actions. Earn trust slowly.
10. ❌ **Not updating this document** — A business model document that's 6 months out of date is worse than none.
